# Historical Context - "The North Star"

The exhibit uses Harriet Tubman and the North Star as a museum-style story about navigation, danger, and freedom. The animatronic is designed for a young audience: simple motion, clear light cues, and a first-person narration that makes the historical moment understandable without turning it into a lecture.

## Why Harriet Tubman

Harriet Tubman was born Araminta Ross in Maryland, escaped slavery, and returned repeatedly to help others escape through the Underground Railroad. The project uses her story because it connects naturally to navigation, secrecy, courage, and the physical symbolism of following a fixed point in the dark.

The competition theme was a "Time Travelers' Museum," set against the United States' 250th anniversary of independence. The exhibit's framing question is deliberately not celebratory: independence for whom? In 1776 and still in 1849, that promise did not extend to millions of people. Tubman did not wait for it to be extended; she left, and then went back for others.

## Why 1849, and Why She Is Young

The figure is set around 1849, roughly the time of Tubman's own escape north, when she was about 27. This is a deliberate departure from the familiar photographic image of her as an elderly woman, which is how most museum depictions present her.

That choice drove production decisions:

- The narration is voiced plain and unprocessed by a young female voice. No voice changer, no performed "old woman" voice. A fake elderly voice would have been historically wrong for 1849 and would have degraded badly through a 3 W speaker in a loud hall.
- The delivery is quiet, slow, and close — the register of someone giving directions in the dark while trying not to be heard — rather than theatrical.

## Why the North Star

The North Star works as both history and mechanism:

- The orange/amber LED on the backdrop gives the audience a visible destination.
- The syringe-driven arm points toward that light during the narration.
- The lantern and night-sky backdrop frame the exhibit as a guided escape scene.

## Presentation Choices

- **Motion:** Head turn, lantern arm, and syringe-driven pointing arm make the figure feel active without overcomplicating the build.
- **Sound:** The DFPlayer narration lets the figure "speak" directly to children.
- **Light:** The lantern and North Star LEDs make the story legible in a dark museum setting.
- **Reveal:** The removable shell allows judges to see that the movement is produced by simple physical mechanisms, not hidden manual control.

## Accuracy Boundary

The exhibit is a symbolic educational scene, not a claim that one specific recorded Tubman escape looked exactly like the presentation. The mechanical choices support the theme while keeping the project buildable and reliable for competition.

The narration deliberately avoids depicting violence or suffering. It is written for a child in a museum: she is a guide, not a spectacle, and the whole message compresses to one line a seven-year-old can carry out of the room — when you feel lost, find your light and keep walking. The 33-second runtime was chosen with the same audience in mind.
